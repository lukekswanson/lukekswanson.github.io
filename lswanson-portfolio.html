The Challenge

Getting paid shouldn't be this hard

Small business owners using QuickBooks need to get paid — it's one of the most critical jobs in the product. But the path from signing up for Payments to sending a first transaction was fragmented, jargon-heavy, and anxiety-inducing. Onboarding flows used financial and legal language that made merchants feel like they might do something wrong. The invoice panel was overloaded with settings and hard to navigate. And as the product expanded to include AI-generated suggestions, recurring payments, and a Mailchimp integration, the content challenges multiplied.

My mandate: lead content design end-to-end — from onboarding architecture through AI assistant voice — across one of QuickBooks' highest-impact product areas.

Discovery

Following merchants through their first transactions

I embedded with the research team to run usability sessions with small business owners at every stage of the payments journey — from first signup through ongoing use. I tracked where merchants paused, re-read, or dropped off, and catalogued the specific language that created confusion or doubt.

Key findings: merchants didn't understand which payment method to choose first, the TRON (KYC/verification) flow used compliance language that felt like a legal trap, and the invoice panel's settings were ordered for internal logic rather than how owners actually thought about their work. Usability testing showed 6 of 7 participants prioritized Payments as the most important feature — but few could complete setup without help.

"I don't know which one to pick. What if I pick the wrong one and can't change it?" — Merchant, usability session on payment method selection

Strategy

A segmented content system across the merchant lifecycle

Rather than rewriting screens in isolation, I built a content framework organized around where a merchant was in their journey: signed up but not yet verified, verified but no transactions yet, first transaction sent, first payment received, ongoing use. Each segment got targeted messaging — a contextual AI agent welcome, proactive tips, and clear next actions — rather than generic prompts.

For the invoice panel, I drove a structural rethink: moving from a WYSIWYG settings panel that overwhelmed users to a unified panel organized by how merchants think about invoices. This included consolidating redundant settings, surfacing payment options more prominently, and introducing a hierarchy that put the most-used controls first.

For the AI assistant (Intuit Assist), I defined the voice principles for agentic content: first-person where it helps ("$1,000 worth of invoices are ready for your review"), passive voice where the agent's presence could feel intrusive, and a strict filter on what the AI should and shouldn't surface proactively.

Execution

Writing in context, shipping at scale

I worked directly in Figma with product designers across multiple concurrent workstreams: the Payments onboarding flow (TRON), the invoicing panel redesign, the AI-powered invoice assistant, late fees, recurring payments, and a Mailchimp payment link integration.

For the AI assistant, I wrote and iterated copy for every state: empty states before a customer is selected, proactive suggestions based on merchant data (overdue invoices, payment patterns), and the late fees feature — where the AI analyzes invoice history and recommends whether to set up a policy. Each suggestion needed to feel helpful and earned, not pushy.

For Mailchimp, I defined the content model for a new payment link block inside email campaigns — including the empty state before a link exists, the "Create a payment link" modal (with legal-friendly copy for payment method disclosures), and the success states. This required close collaboration with legal to translate compliance requirements into plain language merchants could actually read.

Measurable impact across the payments journey

Content improvements shipped across Payments onboarding, Invoicing, AI assistant features, and the Mailchimp integration — affecting millions of small business owners. The invoice panel redesign and AI guidance reduced decision friction at key moments in the merchant journey.

The Challenge

Nobody had written the rules yet

As Intuit moved rapidly into GenAI — building AI-powered suggestions, agentic experiences, and LLM-generated content across QuickBooks products — the content design team had a critical gap: there were no established principles for how AI should speak, what it should and shouldn't say proactively, or how writers should work alongside AI-generated output.

Writers across the org were making inconsistent decisions. Engineers were writing prompt templates without content review. And the broader team lacked a shared vocabulary for what "good" GenAI content even looked like. I led the effort to change that — building the guidance, training, and governance infrastructure from scratch.

Discovery

Auditing what we had — and what was missing

I started by auditing existing AI-generated content across QuickBooks products: invoice reminders, agentic suggestions, chat responses, and proactive tips. The problems were consistent: AI spoke in third person when first person felt more natural, over-explained rather than acting, front-loaded too much information, and used tone that felt either too robotic or too casual.

I also reviewed the prompt engineering infrastructure — including the Python codebase behind invoice reminder generation — to understand where content decisions were being made in code rather than by writers. This meant I needed to write guidance that worked not just for designers, but for the engineers building the prompts.

The biggest insight: most AI content failures weren't tone problems — they were cognitive load problems. Agents were saying too much, too soon, to people who just wanted the job done.

Strategy

Three layers: principles, patterns, training

I structured the work across three layers. First, core principles: how agents should speak (first-person where helpful, passive where not), what they should prioritize (customer benefit and task completion over showcasing AI capability), and what they should never do (unsolicited education, exhaustive lists, detailed info about future AI actions).

Second, content patterns: reusable guidance for specific AI content types — agentic welcome messages, proactive suggestions, error recovery, confirmation states, and "explain this" disclosures. Each pattern included worked examples of the wrong and right approach.

Third, writer enablement: training materials the whole content org could use to make consistent decisions without coming to me for every call. This included visual slides that made abstract principles concrete — sentence case vs. title case, "worth the space" vs. not — designed to be engaging enough to actually be remembered.

Execution

From doc to living system

The guidance document — "Content for GBSG Intuit Assist and agentic experiences" — became the canonical reference for AI content decisions across the Payments and Invoicing team. It covered voice principles, cognitive load rules, disclosure patterns, and specific guidance on when agents should speak and when they should stay quiet.

Core principles doc — voice, cognitive load, and agentic content rules

"Worth the space" framework — what agents should and shouldn't say

I developed the training materials as a series of visual, concrete slides that writers and PMs could absorb quickly. Topics included sentence case (the most-violated rule in the product), what content earns its place in an AI response, and before/after examples of agentic copy. The goal was always the same: make the right choice the obvious choice.

Agentic voice — first-person rules and cognitive load principles

I also worked with engineering to embed content review into the prompt engineering workflow — reviewing and rewriting LLM prompt templates for the invoice reminder system, which generates personalized payment request emails at scale. This meant translating content principles into prompt constraints that the model could reliably follow.

Key principle: "Stay focused on the task at hand and customer benefit. But if you find yourself reaching for a pronoun, use 'I' to represent an agentic voice." — from the core principles doc

Outcomes

A content org that can move with AI — not behind it

The guidance and training I built became the foundation for how the QuickBooks content team approaches AI features. Writers and PMs across the org now have a shared language and set of principles — which means fewer inconsistent decisions, faster reviews, and better AI content at launch rather than as a retrofit.
